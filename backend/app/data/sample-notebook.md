# How Learning Sticks: A Short Guide

A sample chapter written for Mindmesh, so a new notebook has something to explore straight away. It covers a few well-studied ideas about remembering what you learn and using it under pressure, such as in an exam or an interview.

## 1. Working Memory and Cognitive Load

Working memory is the part of the mind that holds what you are thinking about right now. It is small: most people can juggle only about four new pieces of information at once. Anything beyond that spills over and is lost.

Cognitive load is the amount of effort a task puts on working memory. Intrinsic load comes from the material itself; a topic with many interacting parts is hard however it is taught. Extraneous load comes from the way the material is presented, such as cluttered slides or jumping between a diagram and its explanation. Good study removes extraneous load so that working memory is free for the material.

Chunking reduces load. When several pieces are learned together as one meaningful unit, they take up a single slot in working memory. An expert reads "supply and demand" as one idea where a beginner sees two separate words and a relationship to work out.

## 2. Long-Term Memory and Schemas

Long-term memory has no practical limit, but information only becomes useful when it can be found again. Knowledge is stored as schemas: organised structures that connect a new idea to things you already know.

A schema builds on prior knowledge. New material that links to an existing schema is learned faster and remembered longer than material that stands alone. This is why understanding the prerequisites of a topic matters: without them, the new idea has nothing to attach to.

## 3. The Forgetting Curve

Memories fade quickly after first learning unless they are used. Most of what is forgotten is lost in the first few days. The forgetting curve describes this drop: recall falls steeply at first and then levels off.

Each time a memory is successfully recalled, the curve becomes flatter, so the memory lasts longer before the next review is needed.

## 4. Retrieval Practice

Retrieval practice means pulling information out of memory rather than putting it back in. Answering a question from memory, explaining an idea without notes, or taking a short quiz are all forms of retrieval practice.

Retrieval practice builds on the forgetting curve: the effort of recalling something that has started to fade strengthens the memory more than rereading it. Rereading feels productive because the text looks familiar, but familiarity is not the same as being able to recall.

Feedback makes retrieval practice work. Checking an answer against the source corrects mistakes before they are learned.

## 5. Spaced Repetition

Spaced repetition builds on both the forgetting curve and retrieval practice. Instead of studying a topic many times in one sitting, reviews are spread out, with the gap growing after each successful recall: a day, then three days, then a week, then a month.

Spacing beats cramming for anything you need to remember for more than a few days. Cramming can get you through tomorrow's test, but most of it is gone a week later.

## 6. Interleaving

Interleaving means mixing different kinds of problems in one practice session instead of doing all of one kind before moving on. It feels harder, and practice scores are often lower, but performance on a later test is better.

Interleaving builds on retrieval practice. Because each problem may be of a different type, you must first recognise which method applies, which is exactly the skill a real exam or interview demands.

## 7. Elaboration and the Feynman Technique

Elaboration means explaining an idea in your own words and connecting it to examples and to other ideas. Asking "why does this work?" and "how is this like something I already know?" builds richer schemas.

The Feynman technique is a simple form of elaboration: explain a concept as if teaching someone new to it, notice where the explanation breaks down, go back to the source to fill the gap, and explain it again more simply.

## 8. From Knowing to Showing

Knowing an idea and being able to show it are different skills. In an interview, you have to retrieve the idea quickly, choose the parts that matter for the question, and explain them clearly while someone listens.

Practising out loud builds on elaboration and retrieval practice together. A useful structure for answers about your experience is situation, task, action and result: what the context was, what you had to do, what you actually did, and what changed because of it.

Confidence follows evidence. Keeping track of which ideas you can explain and which ones still feel shaky turns vague worry into a short list of things to practise next.
