Six visual directions for Mindmesh

Each direction is shown on the same two screens: Today on a phone, where retention is won or lost, and the gap report on desktop, which is the proposed front door for new users. Directions A to C use bright, saturated colour on a clean base. Direction D is the clean, minimal, premium alternative, E uses a palette sampled from the snack packaging you shared, and F takes its cues from the frosted-glass app photo. All six share the same content and structure from the UX research; only the visual language changes.

All text and colour pairs meet WCAG AA contrast, and no state is shown by colour alone. The fonts are ones already installed on Mac and Windows, so this page renders exactly as intended. A production build would license the final typeface.

A. Highlighter

The product's promise is that every claim is backed by evidence, so the brand is the act of highlighting it. A white page and deep indigo ink keep everything calm. Marker yellow appears in only one place: on the evidence itself, whether that's a quote from your resume or a sentence in your textbook. Blue means action and proven skills, and pink means a gap.

The bold element is the highlight. Everything else stays quiet.

Recommendation

Build on B, Mesh, borrowing one idea from A. The colour fields give Mindmesh a brand people will recognise, and they solve a real navigation problem by showing where you are. The skill mesh is the strongest retention device of the three, and it's honest because nodes only fill from real answers. From A, keep the marker-yellow highlight for evidence quotes in the reader and the gap report. It's the clearest way to show that every claim is backed by a source.

If the brand should feel premium rather than playful, choose D, Atelier instead. It's the best fit for senior professionals and for any later move into paid coaching or employer partnerships. A good middle path is to use D's restraint as the base and bring in B's area colours only as thin accents, for example a slim tangerine rule on Today and violet on Prep. That keeps the wayfinding benefit without the loud fields.

If you want a warmer, everyday consumer feel, E, Pantry, is the friendliest option, especially for students and early-career users. It works best for the mobile Today screen, and it pairs well with D's quieter desktop layout for the gap report.

If you want premium and modern with some warmth, F, Aura, sits between D and the bright directions: soft and calm like D, but with colour and a vivid orange action. It's the strongest fit for the mobile app, provided it has a solid, no-blur fallback for older phones and accessibility settings.

Before committing, test B, C, D, E and F with the 8–10 target users in the research plan. Show each person the gap report in each style and ask which they'd trust with their resume and which they'd open again tomorrow. Trust is the deciding question for senior professionals, and it's the one place C might lose.
